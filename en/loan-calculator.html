    Loan calculator

    Enter the amount borrowed, the annual interest rate and the term in years to see the monthly payment, what you pay in total and how much of that is interest.

    Runs locally in your browser

    How it works

    The monthly payment comes from the standard amortising-loan formula M = P × i ÷ (1 − (1 + i)^−n), where P is the amount borrowed, i is the annual rate divided by 100 and then by 12 (a monthly rate) and n is the number of monthly payments. Early payments are mostly interest and later ones are mostly principal, which is why the total you repay is higher than the amount you borrowed. The calculation assumes a fixed rate for the whole term, the same payment every month, no missed payments and no fees, insurance or early-repayment charges.

    Worked example

    Borrowing 10,000 at 6% a year over 5 years: i = 0.06 ÷ 12 = 0.005 and n = 60. The payment is 10,000 × 0.005 ÷ (1 − 1.005^−60) = 193.33. Sixty payments of 193.33 make 11,599.80 in total, so the interest is 1,599.80.

    Questions and answers

    Is this financial advice?

    No. It is arithmetic on the three numbers you type in. It does not know your lender's fees, your credit terms or whether the rate will change, so treat the result as an estimate of the payment structure, not an offer.

    Why is the total higher than the amount I borrowed?

    Because interest is charged on the balance that is still outstanding each month. Over a long term the interest adds up even at a modest rate, and the figure shown is the sum of every payment.

    What if the interest rate is zero?

    Then the payment is simply the amount divided by the number of months, and the total equals the amount borrowed. The tool handles that case without dividing by zero.

    Does it include fees or insurance?

    No. Arrangement fees, payment protection, insurance and late charges are outside the calculation. Add them to the amount borrowed if you want them reflected, or treat the result as a lower bound.

    Why do longer terms cost more overall?

    Spreading the same balance over more months lowers each payment but charges interest for longer, so the total interest rises even though the monthly figure falls.
