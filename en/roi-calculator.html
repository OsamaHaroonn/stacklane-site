    ROI calculator

    Enter what an investment cost and what it is worth now to see the profit or loss, the return as a percentage and the value as a multiple of what you put in.

    Runs locally in your browser

    How it works

    Return on investment is (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100, so a result of 60% means you ended up with 1.60 for every 1 you started with. A loss shows as a loss row with a positive money figure and a negative percentage, which keeps the arithmetic honest: you can always see which direction the result went. The initial value must be above zero because dividing by zero has no answer. Nothing here accounts for fees, tax, inflation or the length of time you held the investment — a 60% return over one year and a 60% return over ten years are treated the same.

    Worked example

    Bought for 250 and sold for 400: the profit is 400 − 250 = 150, and 150 ÷ 250 × 100 = 60%, so the return is 60% and the multiple is 400 ÷ 250 = 1.60. Bought for 250 and sold for 200: the loss is 50, which is 50 ÷ 250 × 100 = −20%, and the multiple is 0.80.

    Questions and answers

    Is this financial advice?

    No. It is arithmetic on two numbers you typed in. It does not know your tax position, your fees or your objectives, so treat the result as a calculation, not a recommendation.

    Does it include fees and taxes?

    No. Trading fees, commissions, currency charges and tax all reduce the real return. Deduct them from the final value first if you want them reflected.

    Why does a loss show a negative percentage but a positive money figure?

    So the direction is never ambiguous. The row is labelled as a loss, the money figure is the size of that loss, and the percentage keeps its minus sign because that is how the percentage is signed.

    Why must the initial amount be above zero?

    The return is divided by the amount you invested. With nothing invested the division has no answer, so the tool asks for a real figure instead of printing Infinity.

    How is this different from annual return?

    This is the total return over the whole period. Annual return would divide it across the years held, and would compound if you re-invested. Time held is deliberately not an input here.
