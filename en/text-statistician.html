    Text

    Text statistician

    Paste any text for a statistical breakdown: counts, average sentence length, a Flesch reading ease score and the words that carry the most weight.

    Runs locally in your browser

    How it works

    Words are runs of letters and numbers, so hyphenated and apostrophised forms count once. Sentences are split on . ! ? and the ellipsis. Paragraphs are split on blank lines. Average sentence length is words divided by sentences. Flesch reading ease is 206.835 minus 1.015 times the average sentence length minus 84.6 times syllables per word: higher is easier, and a score in the sixties is plain English. The word ranking ignores closed-class words such as “the” and “of”, which appear in almost every sentence and say nothing about the subject.

    Worked example

    A 120-word paragraph of eight sentences averages 15 words per sentence. With about 180 syllables it scores roughly 62 on Flesch, which reads as plain English. Its ranking might lead with “invoice”, “payment” and “client”.

    Questions and answers

    Is the text sent anywhere?

    No. Everything is counted in this tab and nothing is stored. Closing the page discards the text.

    What does the Flesch score mean?

    It estimates how easy a passage is to read on a 0–100 scale: 90–100 is very easy, 60–70 plain English, 30–50 difficult and below 30 very difficult. It was built for English and is only a rough guide for other languages.

    Why are common words missing from the ranking?

    Words like “the”, “and” and “of” are excluded on purpose. They are the most frequent words in almost any English text, so including them would hide the words that actually describe the subject. They still count towards every total.

    Do the counts work for non-Latin scripts?

    Character and word counts use Unicode letter and number properties, so Arabic, Cyrillic and Greek are counted correctly. The syllable estimate behind Flesch assumes Latin spelling, so treat that score as indicative only outside English.
