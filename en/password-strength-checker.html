    Password strength checker

    Type a password to see how many bits of entropy it represents, which character classes it uses, and how the estimate is calculated.

    Runs locally in your browser

    How it works

    The estimate assumes every character was picked at random from the set the password draws on. That set, the pool, is the sum of the classes in use: 26 for lowercase, 26 for uppercase, 10 for digits, 32 for punctuation and one more for a space. Entropy is the length multiplied by the base-two logarithm of the pool size, which is the same as length × log(pool) ÷ log(2). The bar shows the result against 128 bits and the labels use fixed thresholds printed with the result: under 28 very weak, under 36 weak, under 60 fair, under 80 strong, 80 and above very strong. This is an estimate, not a measurement and not a prediction of how long a guess would take.

    Worked example

    For Tr0ub4dor&3 the length is 11 and all four classes are present, so the pool is 26 + 26 + 10 + 32 = 94. The base-two logarithm of 94 is 6.5546 bits per character, and 11 × 6.5546 = 72.10 bits, which the tool labels strong because it is between 60 and 80.

    Questions and answers

    Is this a crack-time estimate?

    No. It reports entropy on the assumption that each character was chosen uniformly at random from the pool. A password made of two dictionary words has far less real entropy than its character count suggests.

    Why 32 symbols?

    That is the count of punctuation characters on a standard US keyboard layout. A space adds one more to the pool because it is a separate, usually unexpected character.

    What do the labels mean?

    They are this tool's fixed bands: under 28 bits very weak, 28 to 35 weak, 36 to 59 fair, 60 to 79 strong, and 80 or more very strong.

    Is my password sent anywhere or stored?

    No. It is read in this page, used for the arithmetic and never written to storage, a cookie, the URL or the console. Reloading loses it immediately.

    Does a long passphrase score well?

    The figure is an estimate that assumes each character is picked at random. A phrase built from real words is far weaker than its length suggests, because the words come from a small dictionary an attacker can try first. For reference, 20 characters drawn at random from 26 lowercase letters would give 20 × log₂(26) = 94.01 bits; a 20-character sentence of ordinary words does not.
